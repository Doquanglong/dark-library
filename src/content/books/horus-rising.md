# Synopsis

It is the 31st millennium. Under the benevolent leadership of the Immortal Emperor, the Imperium of Man has stretched out across the galaxy. It is a golden age of discovery and conquest. But now, on the eve of victory, the Emperor leaves the front lines, entrusting the great crusade to his favourite son, Horus. Promoted to Warmaster, can the idealistic Horus carry out the Emperor's grand plan, or will this promotion sow the seeds of heresy amongst his brothers? Horus Rising is the first chapter in the epic tale of the Horus Heresy, a galactic civil war that threatened to bring about the extinction of humanity.

# Chapters

## 1 · Blood from misunderstanding

Garviel Loken has a story he keeps being asked to retell: "I was there, the day Horus slew the Emperor." It is a joke with a bitter edge. In the 203rd year of the Great Crusade, about a year after Ullanor, the 63rd Expedition is thrown off course by a warp storm and stumbles into a system of nine worlds ruled by a man calling himself the Emperor of Mankind — a human ruler who kept his little empire alive through Old Night and now demands the newcomers swear fealty to him.

Horus, newly made Warmaster and still uneasy in the title, sends his favourite captain Hastur Sejanus to greet him. Sejanus suggests there might be another Emperor, and is cut down in the throne room by the ruler's elite guard, the Invisibles. Horus grieves but offers a second chance; Maloghurst's envoy shuttles are shot out of the sky, and six hundred hidden warships rise to fight. Abaddon makes one last plea, is fired on, and Horus gives the order: illuminate him, spare what you can, avenge Sejanus.

The Luna Wolves storm the High City behind Titans and thousands of Army troops. Loken leads Tenth Company in, thinking how sad it is that these people cannot see they are the same species and not the enemy. The fighting is nothing like Ullanor — this is a modern, civilised enemy with matching technology, and Loken judges the only real difference is Astartes flesh. Abaddon jokes over the vox that whoever reaches the false Emperor first gets to kill him. Loken clears a hidden automatic sentry gun that has already torn Brother Zakias apart, and pushes into the state apartments with his oldest friend Nero Vipus and Locasta Tactical Squad.

## 2 · Meeting the Invisibles

The frame story: the remembrancer Mersadie Oliton, sent by Captain Torgaddon, interviews Loken in his arming chamber and asks him to tell it. Inside the palace, the Invisibles ambush them — elite guards using field technology that bends light to hide them and projects crushing force as a weapon. Several battle-brothers are killed outright before Loken learns to shoot at the blemishes in the air. Vipus loses his right hand and cuts it off himself to keep fighting.

Loken climbs alone up a vast phototropic tower, ring platform by ring platform, to the very top, where a golden throne sits empty and an elderly man in mauve robes defies him. The old man argues the point that will haunt Loken: if their philosophies differ, why not simply pass by and leave them alone? Loken answers that truth itself is amoral; the old man replies that in serving that truth he makes himself immoral. He refuses to surrender to anyone but the overall commander, and insists Horus come in person.

Catulan Reaver Squad teleports up. Ekaddon wants to kill the old man; Loken overrules him and has a signal sent. Then Loken realises the trap — the old man was stalling to bring Horus here, and the real "Emperor" has been sitting invisible on the throne the whole time. Ekaddon shoots the old man off the platform. The throne unleashes a shockwave that blows most of Catulan out of the tower and leaves Loken hanging over the drop. Horus teleports in, a giant in white gold with an eye on his breastplate, stands unmoved in the storm of force, and kills the hidden imposter with a single shot: "So will I deal with all tyrants and deceivers." He pulls Loken up and tells him not to be so formal. Back in the present, Mersadie asks what Horus is like, and Loken coldly corrects her for using his name without title.

## 3 · Replevin

Three months on, the first remembrancers arrive — an order created by the Emperor after Ullanor and countersigned by Malcador, sending millions of artists, poets and imagists out with the expedition fleets to record the crusade. They get a cold welcome. The martial class resents the Emperor's withdrawal to Terra, resents the new civilian Council of Terra that has supplanted the War Council, and takes it out on the newcomers. Nobody questions Horus's appointment as Warmaster; they question why a proxy was needed at all.

On the conquered world, replevin proceeds — the empire is dismantled and parcelled out, with Lord Commander Varvaras's Army mopping up. Horus grants the dead "Emperor" a state funeral as a gesture toward compliance rather than pure conquest. Abaddon and others refuse to attend the funeral of Sejanus's killer, so Rogal Dorn, travelling with the fleet for talks, has his Imperial Fists line the route instead so the Luna Wolves need not tarnish their honour. Remembrancers are barred from attending. We meet three of them: the poet Ignace Karkasy, the imagist Euphrati Keeler, and Mersadie Oliton, freshly stung by Loken.

Torgaddon comes to the blade-school, spars with Loken, and — through a stream of jokes and false denials — tells him the truth: Sejanus's place in the Mournival is his, seconded by Abaddon and decided by the Warmaster himself. Loken then seeks out the Primary Iterator Kyril Sindermann, whose lecture argues that mankind's greatest achievement was casting off religion, that the iterators must never become liars, and that they are right not because they are mighty. Loken confesses the doubt Mersadie planted: could they not have left these people alone? Sindermann answers with the parable of the drowning boy, tells him a weapon must not think but that Loken's humanity is exactly why the Mournival needs him — the Mournival's job is to be the Warmaster's conscience and the voices he listens to first, and only a weapon that questions its use is any good in that role.

## 4 · Summoned

Mersadie Oliton is granted a transit permit to the surface of the conquered world — now officially designated Sixty-Three Nineteen, since the locals call it Terra and there cannot be two. Her escort is the young iterator Memed and four Army troopers. It is her first time ever setting foot on another world, and she means to make that the theme of her account. She has been summoned personally by Loken, who tells her he was too hard on her last time, and that her impertinent question is the very reason he is here tonight. He will not let her witness what follows, but promises to convey some of it later if he judges it appropriate. She waits on the lawn and teaches the soldiers a card game Karkasy gave her.

Beyond the hedge, in a shell-broken water garden turned into a shallow lake, Loken wades out to an islet where three figures wait in lantern light: Abaddon, Torgaddon and "Little Horus" Aximand — so called because his face uncannily resembles the Warmaster's. Loken learns he is the first man ever elected to the Mournival who is not one of the "Sons of Horus" who share that resemblance; he owes his looks to plain Cthonian stock, so he has got there on merit alone.

The ritual unsettles him — the moon must witness it, and its reflection must be caught in water — and he says plainly that he is a man of Imperial Truth and will bow to no spirit. They laugh and reassure him: it is pantomime, an old habit kept up because it makes the moment matter. The oath itself is secular, man to man, a brotherhood only death can break. He is told the Mournival has no rank among its four, that he must call them Ezekyle, Horus and Tarik, and that he should pick whoever he actually trusts to run Tenth Company in his absence rather than the man seniority demands. The nine dead members are named, Sejanus last. Loken swears to the Mournival, to the Warmaster, to the Emperor, to the truth of the Imperium "no matter what evil may assail it," and to his brothers no matter the cost. Meditating on the oath, he reflects that Astartes are effectively immortal but never get to enjoy it, because war always collects. They brand the crescent of the new moon above his helm's right eye — Aximand carries the half, Torgaddon the gibbous, Abaddon the full.

Outside, Memed teaches the card players an ancient game and explains that the winning hand of four royal cards — ace, monarch, empress, knave — is called a mournival. A flare bangs off behind the hedge at that exact moment, and Mersadie realises she has just been handed her future.

## 5 · Peeter Egon Momus

The expedition's architect designate presents his grand plans for rebuilding the High City to a hot, resentful crowd of vetted local dignitaries and newly permitted remembrancers. He is inaudible, the hololiths are washed out by the sun, and two Imperial Fists stand behind him as silent authority — the architects of war watching over the architect of peace. Ignace Karkasy, bored and parched, slips away past a soldier who warns him the back streets aren't secure: officially the world is days from compliance, but there are insurgents, or at least a lot of people who have lost everything and aren't happy.

Alone in the ruined streets for the first time in a year, Karkasy finds his muse again in the graffiti covering every wall — curses, calls for revenge, lists of the dead, pleas for news of the missing, transcribed sacred texts. He copies them into one of his precious Bondsman Number 7 notebooks, of which he has only nine left and fears they may represent the limit of his talent. He drinks in a silent local eating house where nobody will speak to him, and concludes that five thousand years of separation has grown these people into something biologically identical but culturally alien, a graft raised under another sun.

He then wanders into an untouched temple and finds an altar and an old illuminated book, and realises what marks these people out: they still believe. That leads him to the Lectitio Divinitatus, the underground missal pushing the Cult of the Emperor against the Emperor's own explicit refusal of godhood — five thousand years of war to expunge all gods, and now the man who did it is being made into one. He wonders aloud whether humanity is simply cursed to crave something that does not exist.

Drunk, he tries to write, tears out the pages as terrible, and is stoned by local boys. Back at the eating house he tells a squad of Army troopers — who have been burning slogans off the walls — that Momus's city will not last, that everything man builds comes apart, and that the Imperium will fall asunder as soon as it is constructed. Enraged, the soldiers beat him to the floor while their officer tries to pull them off. By the time he succeeds, Karkasy has stopped talking, and stopped breathing.

## 6 · Counsel

Loken's first Mournival duty is a briefing on the strategium. Torgaddon warns him quietly to be ready to catch a question. They pass through the Avenue of Glory and Lament, where the names of every fallen crusader are painted in gold — the newest names from the High City still being stencilled in.

Horus presides, standing on a plain ouslite dais with no throne. He is warm, funny and overwhelming, and he begins by lavishing praise on the terrified Peeter Egon Momus, arguing that what the crusade builds matters more than what it strikes down and that they must leave legacies, not just conquests. Regulus of the Mechanicum reports captured technologies and three recovered STC patterns. Governor Elect Rakris and Lord Commander Varvaras report that a stubborn insurgency in the southern hemisphere means the world cannot be certified compliant for years.

Horus lets the problem hang and looks at Abaddon. That is the question. Loken steps out of the shadows and argues that the speartip failed to finish its job and that Tenth Company should be sent to end the insurgency quickly rather than let a guerrilla war grind on for years. Varvaras and Rakris agree; Horus makes it a command, and praises Loken for cutting to the quick.

Maloghurst, "the Twisted," then makes his first public appearance since being found alive, now a hero thanks to Euphrati Keeler's picts, and lays out three regions for the expedition's next move: an empty resource cluster, a region bearing the Old Terran name Sagittarius that may hold a non-human interstellar culture, and a region eighteen weeks away where Khitas Frome's 140th Expedition of Blood Angels has run into a xenos war and gone quiet. Horus stops smiling at that, and says he will speak to his brother Sanguinius. Maloghurst also insists publicly on keeping his mocking nickname — his body is broken, his mind is not.

Afterwards Loken meets Rogal Dorn, who has been travelling with the fleet to advise Horus on the Warmaster's role. Dorn explains the primarchs' varied reactions to Horus's elevation, and admits that the fact of himself frightens him — the Emperor had no like, so he made demi-gods for company. Sigismund tells Loken bluntly that the crusade will never end and that in the far future there will be only war; Loken calls him wrong. Dorn reveals he personally vouched for Loken's election because the Mournival needed a naysmith: Sedirae would say yes for the glory, Iacton Qruze would say yes to stay in favour, and Horus needs an honest, dissenting voice. Then he delivers the chapter's sting — Loken should understand what Horus made him do that morning. The Warmaster had primed the Mournival, because he is cultivating the image of a peacemaker and wanted somebody other than himself to be the one who proposed unleashing the Legion.

## 7 · Oaths of moment

Euphrati Keeler and five other remembrancers are given ten guarded minutes on the embarkation deck, and she ignores the rules and shoots anyway. She hears in passing about a remembrancer who slipped his minders on the surface and caused a scandal that nearly got the whole contingent suspended — Karkasy, though she doesn't know it, and he is evidently alive. Mersadie Oliton has inexplicably missed her slot.

Three hundred Luna Wolves march aboard the Stormbirds. The iterator Emont explains the oath of moment: beyond their permanent oath to the Emperor, Astartes swear a specific, ritual oath binding them to the particular action in front of them. Loken kneels to take his last of all, sworn on a bolter, heard by Sedirae and witnessed by Targost — with the whole Mournival present to see it done right. Keeler photographs the moment, and Sindermann later tells her it will be reproduced in history texts for as long as the Imperium endures.

Sindermann and Maloghurst then grant the remembrancers permission to follow the strike force down to the surface. Maloghurst argues for it as honest propaganda: the truth cannot be wrong even when it is ugly, and a mature culture must be able to look at what it does. Sindermann will accompany them himself, on the Warmaster's stipulation. Keeler notes wryly that they will still only be shown what someone chooses to show them.

On the flight down, Loken chases a vox fault. When the flight officer washes the signal clean, a tiny, dry voice comes through the static: Samus — that is the only name they will hear, it means the end and the death, Samus is all around you, Samus is the man beside you, Samus will gnaw upon your bones, look out, Samus is here. The flight officer is visibly afraid, which disgusts Loken, who has no capacity for fear. He decides the enemy is simply trying to scare them.

## 8 · One-way war

The insurgents have fortified a mountain fastness in the Whisperheads, in the deeply superstitious southern highlands. Loken's officers gather; he has already given the proxy command of Tenth to Nero Vipus, and Sergeant Xavyer Jubal of first squad is openly aggrieved at being passed over. Loken salves him by giving Hellebore the weight of the first strike behind Rassek's Terminators.

An Army officer who has bled here for six weeks warns Loken that the place is haunted — the Whisperheads are named for voices heard when nobody is there. Loken chides him for admitting to a belief in spirits, and dismisses "Samus" as good enemy propaganda.

Below, the remembrancers are parked in a safe town and shown nothing. Sindermann consults the archive and finds the answer: in local myth the Whisperheads are the one place where the spirit world touches this one, and Samus is the chief devil, imprisoned there long ago by an emperor of legend — their equivalent of Seytan. He finds it charmingly primitive. Keeler tells him she has been hearing Samus hissing at her since they landed, and she doesn't have a vox.

The assault takes sixty-eight minutes. Nine hundred and seventy-two insurgents die; not one Luna Wolf falls. Loken finds no glory in it. He gives a mercy stroke to a dying man who begs to be commended to the gods, telling him gently that there are no gods and that he is simply dying. He orders some thirty meltwater fanes destroyed, and afterwards tells Vipus he pities these people — the crusade is taking away every comfort of mystery and leaving them a hard, brief, unforgiving reality.

Then Jubal calls him on the vox, speaking strangely, saying he has found something and that Samus is here. Loken descends with Brakespur Squad to a stone well over an abyss and finds Jubal alone, the rest of Hellebore gone, insisting there are words written in the falling water. When Loken orders him restrained, Jubal shoots Sergeant Udon through the face, kills two more, and laughs: "I'm Samus. Look out! Samus is here."

## 9 · The unthinkable

Interleaved with this is an earlier interview in which Mersadie presses Loken for feeling rather than fact. He explains that Astartes do not master fear, they simply have it bred out — which she privately thinks strips the heroism out of them and wonders what else was torn out with it. Pushed for wonders, he offers the giant carnival hats of Sarosel; the keylekid, who thought war so abhorrent they confined it to designated slaughterhouses and waited politely for the Imperium to meet them there, and were exterminated for it; and the overseers of Dahinta, deathless thinking machines still repairing the cities of the long-extinct humans who built them, who no longer recognised men because Astartes did not look like their masters. His last story is a perfect twenty-metre map of Terra found ten kilometres underground on a dead alien world, showing coastlines erased ages ago — the one thing that, if he could feel fear, would have frightened him.

Back in the present, Loken faces the truly unthinkable: Astartes killing Astartes. None of Brakespur can bring themselves to fire on a brother, so Loken throws his bolter away and tackles Jubal bare-handed while Jubal guns down more of them and drives his fingers straight through one man's gorget. Jubal's strength is far beyond Astartes limits; he swats Loken clear across the chamber. They duel with combat blades on a narrow, water-slick stone pier over the abyss until Loken runs him through. Loken then risks everything to haul the body back from the edge while his own men ask why he would even want to.

Sindermann is brought up through the fortress and, for the first time in his life, walks a fresh battlefield — the reality behind everything he has iterated for years. He keeps whispering "Terra," and by the time he reaches Loken the word has become "terror." Loken tells him the official line will be no losses, then confidentially shows him Jubal's bound corpse and asks him directly: are spirits real? Sindermann says absolutely not, and constructs a rational explanation — the shrivelled flesh and strange blemishes suggest a virulent contagion that destroyed Jubal's mind, and the ravings were just repeated enemy propaganda. Loken accepts it with relief. Sindermann tells him to quarantine the site.

Then Jubal's corpse lifts its head and says "Look out." He bursts his bonds, swells until his armour shatters, and becomes something bloated, clawed and needle-toothed, flickering with yellow light as if out of phase with the present, drooling that Samus is here. He hurls Loken into the pool and throws Sindermann at Vipus, then shambles out into the tunnels where three remembrancers have crept in against orders. Keeler screams and photographs at the same time. The thing smashes Van Krasten to pieces against the walls and bites away Borodin Flora's head and upper body as he makes the sign of the aquila and cries "Begone!" Keeler sinks to her knees, unable to run, and waits to find out what her death will be.

## 10 · The Warmaster and his son

Loken reports to Abaddon: the Jubal-thing took ninety rounds and a flamer to destroy, and was killed moments before it could bite Keeler in half. Abaddon sets the official line — a clean victory, casualties taken in two squads because the insurgents fought bitterly, and two remembrancers lost in a landing accident. Loken protests that he cannot and will not forget what happened; Abaddon argues it is a matter of security and morale, and that the truth would break the expedition's spirit and tarnish the Legion.

Then Horus himself arrives, in plain foul-weather clothes, and kneels so his eyes are level with Loken's. He reads Loken's own oath of moment back to him and shows that every clause was fulfilled — especially "no matter the ferocity or ingenuity of the foe." Loken did not run; he fought. Shame is cast out.

Horus then gives him the secret explanation. Not spirits, not daemons in the old sense: the warp. He explains that the Imperium could not exist without the warp, that it is used but never mastered, and that there are entities in it that can seep into a man. The old words — daemon, spirit, possessor — are useful labels for extra-dimensional aliens, so long as everyone remembers there are no gods out there, no immutable evil, only inhuman things to be destroyed. The Whisperheads sit close to the warp, which is where the myths came from; the locals used their crude knowledge of it as a weapon, and Jubal's resentment at being passed over left a chink for it to enter. Ing Mae Sing's records confirm a warp spike at the moment of the drop.

Loken asks why they are told so little about the warp. Horus answers that little is known — and then reveals something more personal. He is Warmaster because the Emperor is busy with a higher calling; and when Loken asks what it is, Horus admits the Emperor never told him. "Not even me," he whispers, and Loken senses the wound in his commander's pride. Horus speculates that the Emperor has withdrawn to master the warp itself, because without that the Imperium will founder no matter how many worlds it takes.

Back on the ship, the dead of Brakespur are carried in under Legion banners, and the two dead remembrancers with no flags at all. Mersadie is waiting in Loken's arming chamber to apologise for missing the drop — she was detained helping Ignace Karkasy, who is in serious trouble. Loken tells her to be grateful she missed it. She offers herself as his remembrancer and asks whether he wants to share what happened. He says no.


## 11 · Loathe and love

The scene shifts to Saul Tarvitz of the Emperor's Children, on the surface of a world designated One Forty Twenty and named Murder. The enemy are the megarachnid — huge upright arthropods whose armour is bonded to their shells and whose blade-limbs are natural weapons, a seamless fusion of biology and technology. Tarvitz loathes them for their want of human perfection and loves them as a rival worth measuring the Legion against.

The drop was a disaster: nightmarish atmospherics scattered the pods, and Tarvitz and his fellow captain Lucius have regrouped only about a third of the company with no contact with the fleet or with Lord Commander Eidolon. The landscape is a creaking forest of twenty-metre grey grass stalks over red dust, hung with hissing black larvae, dotted with white thorn-capped "trees." The megarachnid attack in blurs of silver, and the first clash lasts twelve seconds and kills two veterans. Lucius brings back a severed blade-limb — metal skin over chitin over wet meat — and cuts his own gauntlet open just by gripping it.

The chapter also sets out how they got here. The Blood Angels of the 140th Expedition under Captain Khitas Frome went down first and were swallowed. Landers were destroyed by "shield-storms" that rose the instant anything entered the atmosphere, as if the planet's life could command its own weather. Frome's messages grew shorter and more desperate — he admired the enemy's martial schooling, then asked for the full force of the Legion — and his last transmission was three words forced out with inhuman effort: "This. World. Is. Murder." Master August of the fleet fed his forces down piecemeal after him, and none came back.

Eidolon arrived first with a single company, and when August suggested waiting for the Blood Angels relief force and the Luna Wolves four days out, that decided him — he had no intention of sharing the glory of rescuing a rival Legion with the Warmaster's elite, and ordered the drop at once. Tarvitz notes the difference between himself and Lucius: both pursue perfection, but Lucius is ambitious where Tarvitz is content to be a file officer and keep his place perfectly.

Meanwhile the Vengeful Spirit translates in after a twelve-week passage that should have taken eighteen, using powers only a Warmaster can call on. Horus is delighted to have an alien war at last — a clear, simple foe he can fight without restraint, regret or remorse, after the misery of killing humans on Sixty-Three Nineteen. Studying the system display, he notices that the "untranslatable" xenos beacon broadcasts look like music, and orders them run properly, remarking that he doesn't intend to take the 140th's word for anything. Maloghurst confirms the extra ships are Imperial, including the Proudheart with Eidolon's company aboard — who beat them there, and who are, it seems, not doing well.

## 12 · The nature of the enemy

The megarachnid are described in detail: three metres tall, eight limbs, four for running and four ending in metre-long blades, no eyes, and armour plating fused to their bodies. They move so fast that Tarvitz's first kill is completed before the headless body of his comrade Kercort has even hit the ground. Tarvitz's own breastplate is gouged across the palatine aquila — a symbol only the Emperor's Children are permitted to wear — which he takes as a genuine insult.

After ninety hours and twenty-eight engagements, seven of their small party are dead and the advance has become mechanical and trance-like. Tarvitz decides it is an almost perfect war: simple, engrossing, uncomplicated by politics or belief, with no room to slacken. Most of the men switch to blades. Lucius, an unbeaten swordsman with an antique Terran long sword and the captured megarachnid limb as a second blade, treats the fighting as a game, passing up clean kills to prolong it, and talks about capturing live specimens for sparring practice and naming a new sword move "the Lucius." Tarvitz wonders whether the aliens have language, art or emotion, and whether they attack because to them humans are the hideous xenos.

In a bare clearing they find the first hard evidence of the Blood Angels: a Mark III visor lens, popped out of its frame by some unimaginable force. A shield-storm closes in and lightning repeatedly strikes their armour without harming them. Then over a hundred megarachnid charge from all sides. The Astartes form a ring, empty their bolters at ten metres, and go to blades; the three-minute fight kills five more of them.

Then a new form appears — winged clades, glassy wings and hooked head crests, which snatch nine armoured men straight off the ground. Tarvitz cuts Lucius free of one by hacking off his shield straps, dropping them both ten metres. Chasing the fliers, they discover what the white "trees" are for: the taken men are impaled on the stone thorns so the winged forms can crack their armour open and feed. Older corpses hang there too, reduced to bone, with fragments of red plate scattered beneath. They have found the Blood Angels.

## 13 · During the voyage

Aboard the Vengeful Spirit during the twelve-week passage, Loken tracks down Sindermann, who has been avoiding everyone and hiding in the darkest back annexe of the archive reading pre-Unification fiction and poetry for comfort. He admits he has healed in body but not in mind, and the fire has gone out of him for now. He lends Loken The Chronicles of Ursh. When Loken asks what book he was reading so intently and hurriedly reshelved, Sindermann says only: "Bad poetry."

Loken understands that Horus has brought him and Tenth Company along to keep him close and watch him, because of what he has seen; Sindermann calls that wisdom, since it gives him a chance to reforge his courage.

Clearing out the dead men's cells, Loken finds a small silver medal among Jubal's effects — a wolf's head against a crescent moon. Vipus is evasive, then admits it could be a lodge medal. The chapter explains the warrior lodges: secret fraternities absorbed from the world of Davin six decades earlier, quasi-religious in origin, running "perpendicular" to the chain of command, and disapproved of by the Emperor. Loken has always forbidden them in Tenth Company. He asks for anyone with information to come forward — and the next day the medal has vanished from the effects.

He also intervenes for Ignace Karkasy, who is facing being shipped home in disgrace. Karkasy admits what he said that provoked the beating: that the Imperium would not endure, that nothing lasts forever, and that they would be fighting forever just to stay alive. Loken notes an Imperial Fists officer said the same thing and was not sent home. He offers probation on three conditions — reform his habits, submit every single thing he writes to Loken, and keep telling the truth. "Truth is all we have," Loken tells him; without it history cannot judge them fairly.

Karkasy later passes on a data-slate from Euphrati Keeler containing her picts of the oath-taking. She has become reclusive since the Whisperheads and says there is nothing to tell, because the First Captain told her there was nothing to tell. Loken asks Karkasy and Oliton to look after her.

Finally, Aximand comes to Loken privately, holding a silver lodge medal, and asks him — as a personal favour — to attend a lodge meeting as his guest and judge for himself. There is a vacancy, he says, since the Whisperheads. Jubal's vacancy. Loken agrees, because it is Aximand asking.

## 14 · Felling the Murder trees

Tarvitz uses all the party's explosives to destroy the thorn trees rather than leave his brothers skewered, over Lucius's objection that they might need the charges for the war. Privately Tarvitz thinks there is no war to win — Murder has swallowed the Blood Angels and now, thanks to Eidolon's hunger for glory, it has swallowed them too, and all that is left is a last stand.

The blast has two consequences. The first is immediate: the explosion succeeds where the vox failed, and two other scattered groups — Captain Anteus's and Eidolon's own — converge on the smoke, bringing them to about fifty Astartes. Eidolon, seething that the operation has gone badly, refuses to acknowledge any error, calls the use of the charges a waste, orders Lucius to throw away the xenos blade-limb, and punishes Tarvitz's squad by sending them back out to sift the wreckage for Blood Angels relics.

Out at the ruins, Lucius and Tarvitz air their friendship. Lucius argues that the Legion's whole creed — Fulgrim's teaching — is that failures must be acknowledged to be eradicated, and apologises both for siding with Eidolon and for using the alien blade. He also thanks Tarvitz for saving his life, and asks to learn steadiness from him.

Then comes the second consequence, and the chapter's real revelation. Under a suddenly clear night sky, while Eidolon's position is attacked half a kilometre away, hundreds of megarachnid arrive in ordered columns and begin to rebuild the trees. Warrior clades stand guard; shovel-limbed clades clear the rubble in mechanical work gangs; huge new "weaver" clades consume the broken stone and regurgitate fresh material, which smaller clades draw from spinnerets and plaster into new trunks with trowel-like precision. Within ten minutes three new trees are rising. This is not a swarm — it is industry.

Tarvitz refuses to let his work be undone and charges the work gangs with five men. It is suicidal. Lodoroton is beheaded and flensed; Pherost is run through; Lucius is buried under a load of setting cement and pinned helpless while Tarvitz stands over him, unable to spare a moment to cut him free. As they are borne down by sheer numbers, drop pods begin falling out of the sky around them like meteors, and fresh bolter and plasma fire tears into the megarachnid. A white gauntlet edged in black reaches down for Tarvitz. "My name is Tarik," his rescuer says. "Pleased to meet you."

## 15 · Informal formalities

Horus arrives at the 140th Expedition unannounced. Ten Luna Wolves captains descend as an honour guard, and when Horus himself steps out — deliberately not smiling — everyone prostrates themselves except Fleet Master Mathanual August, who is too stunned to move. Asked why he does not bow, August says he can't remember how. Horus immediately kneels to *him*, embraces him, says he likes a man too proud to bend his knees, and wins the whole deck over in a moment.

At the war council, the Mournival plays its assigned role: they savage August and Eidolon's equerry Eshkerrus for feeding an entire expedition into a death trap and for Eidolon's glory-hunting, while Horus plays the merciful conciliator who forgives everyone and takes over. Aximand had briefed Loken beforehand — the Legion is feared anyway, so let the Mournival be the war dogs and carry the rebuke, leaving the Warmaster beloved. "Everyone thinks the Warmaster's keenest talent is as a warrior. No one expects him to be a consummate politician."

Then a hole opens in the shield-storms near the Emperor's Children's landing zone, and vox traffic is detected on the surface: men are still alive. Horus initially refuses to repeat the mistake he has just condemned, but Torgaddon argues for one last chance, and when the vox trace is confirmed he authorises the speartip. Loken hears the four captains' oaths of moment himself, and says goodbye to Sedirae, Targost, Moy and Torgaddon before the drop pods fire.

Interleaved with this is the lodge meeting Loken attended six weeks earlier. He goes armed, half hoping to find proof of a coven he can strike at, half dreading that Aximand is tainted. What he finds instead is Torgaddon, Sedirae, Vipus, Ekaddon, Moy and two dozen others, with Serghar Targost as lodge master. There are no rituals and no daemons — just wine, bread, conversation and bad jokes. They explain the lodge as a place where rank is left at the door, where a file warrior and a captain can speak as equals, and where bonds are formed across the chain of command that make the Legion stronger. It is thousands of years old, predating Unification, and the Warmaster has always known about it and turns a blind eye.

Loken objects that secrecy is the enemy of truth and that the Emperor has ruled against it. Torgaddon answers that the Emperor doesn't understand brotherhood because he calls no one brother — even primarchs are only sons. Ekaddon, a personal rival, admits his hostility and offers his hand. Aximand reveals that the Mournival itself began as a visible extension of the invisible lodge, and that the vacancy Loken fills is Udon's, not Jubal's — and that the real warning sign was that Jubal was a member who had stopped attending, so nobody knew what was in his head. The only rules are that you say nothing outside, and that the answer to any question about lodge business is "I can't say." Loken accepts, but leaves Torgaddon with one worry: a body that gets very good at keeping secrets may end up keeping any kind of secret at all.

## 16 · Chosen instrument

Four companies of Luna Wolves have smashed the megarachnid at the clearing. Torgaddon meets Tarvitz, and then Eidolon. When Eidolon says he'll understand if Torgaddon doesn't bow, Torgaddon says he can't imagine any circumstance in which he would. He backs Eidolon down completely: he is the Warmaster's chosen instrument here, Eidolon wasted half a company out of hunger for glory, and thirty-nine men are left alive. Eidolon promises never to forget the insult. Lucius, who despises Torgaddon, talks of killing him; Tarvitz warns him off, and Lucius laughs as though it were a joke.

Torgaddon then draws out Tarvitz. Tarvitz admits privately that he thought the drop reckless and driven by ambition, but defends his Legion: their ethos is purity, not superiority, modelled on the Emperor. Torgaddon replies that the one thing they should never aspire to is the Emperor's supremacy, and that there is no purity in pride. He also reveals that it was Tarvitz's own vox chatter that persuaded Horus to sanction the rescue at all.

Eidolon has meanwhile claimed to Torgaddon that *he* destroyed the trees and that doing so collapsed the local shield-storm — a genuinely useful theory, but stolen. Bulle speaks out of turn and tells the truth: Tarvitz did it, and Eidolon punished him for it. Tarvitz would have let the lie stand because Eidolon is his lord. Torgaddon protects Bulle from reprisal and takes Tarvitz with him to fell more trees — the first hint of how this world might be beaten.

Aboard the flagship, Loken passes time with Tybalt Marr (jealous that his near-twin Moy drew a place in the speartip) and the tiresome ancient Iacton Qruze, "the Half-heard." Alone, he reads The Chronicles of Ursh and stumbles on an account of the conquest of Xozer, where the defending hierophants unleashed sorcery: plagues of insects, boiling water, men turned to stone — and men who "did blister and recompose them ownselves ynto the terrible likeness of daimons" and turned on their own kin. It is exactly what happened to Jubal, described in a pre-Unification chronicle. Loken wonders whether Sindermann gave him this book deliberately.

Then he finds a second, hidden file on Keeler's data-slate, marked IN CONFIDENCE: picts of the Jubal-thing. He goes, robed and hooded, into the Retreat — the remembrancers' carnival-like social hall — to find her, and Karkasy leads him to her billet, where she is living hard, drinking, and sleeping with an Army trooper called Leef.

Keeler tells him what those weeks did to her: nightmares, waking visions, a small pistol and long hours wondering whether she had the strength to use it. She sent the picts as a cry for help, hoping he would tell her the truth. She then shows him what she found in them — the thing is out of phase with the reality around it, and boosting the contrast reveals a superimposed afterimage of a screaming Astartes. Loken holds to the "wild beast" line, and she lets him.

She explains how she survived: she has found faith. The Emperor, she says, actually does protect — he uses the Astartes and all the martial instruments to shield mankind from truths that would drive it mad, which is why he does not share them. Then she asks Loken the question that hangs over the rest of the book: his loyalty closes ranks around his brothers, so how far does it go? If it happened to one Astartes it can happen to others — how many would have to turn before he broke step and said "This is wrong"? Loken calls it beyond countenance with Horus as Warmaster. She replies that it rather depends where the inconsistencies occur.

After he leaves, Keeler sets out two candles and a small figurine of the Emperor, kneels before the makeshift shrine, and begins to read the crude, misprinted pamphlet Leef gave her: the Lectitio Divinitatus. Loken is recalled to duty — a battle-barge has translated in behind them. Sanguinius has come.

## 17 · Lord of the Angels

Framed by an earlier interview in which Loken describes the Great Triumph after Ullanor to Mersadie: an entire continent planed smooth by the Mechanicum as a stage, a roadway five hundred kilometres long lined with greenskin skulls on iron posts, a hundred thousand Astartes, eight million Army, nine primarchs present, and a hundred thousand warriors weeping as the Emperor announced his retirement from the field.

On Murder, Sanguinius arrives — golden, winged, cheeks marked with ash in mourning, refusing painted ritual tears because he has real ones. Tarvitz personally hands him the recovered Blood Angels relics, and Sanguinius recognises the armourer's mark on a helm and names the dead captain from memory. He thanks Torgaddon most of all. He then rejects vengeance as a motive, but says the megarachnid's implacable hostility is reason enough. Horus answers: "We will murder Murder together."

The war lasts six months. The megarachnid never break, never retreat, and never grow easier to kill. Horus insists on fighting in person, deflecting the Mournival's careful attempts to talk him out of it with mock anger and an appeal to their warrior code — Loken suspects the real reason is that Horus cannot bear to watch Sanguinius fight without him, or be outshone by the brother he loves most. Many reputations are made: Sedirae, Qruze, Tybalt Marr, and Saul Tarvitz, who fights at Torgaddon's side and repairs the Luna Wolves' respect for the Emperor's Children.

Sanguinius, acting as Horus's conscience, warns him not to let a rift open with Fulgrim over Eidolon's arrogance, and suggests that as Warmaster he should do what the Emperor advised at Ullanor: rename the XVI Legion so no one mistakes where authority now lies. Horus refuses. Loken asks Abaddon what name was meant. The answer: the Sons of Horus.

In the sixth month, three enormous silver-white starships translate in, broadcasting the same untranslatable "music" as the beacons. They halt within firing range, and speak in the language of mankind, in a chorus of overlaid voices: "Did you not see the warnings we left? What have you done here?"


## 18 · Make no mistakes

The Imperium becomes the guests of the interex, and immediately voices call for war — Eidolon, Maloghurst, Sedirae, Targost, Goshen, Raldoron. The argument: the interex are lost human kin, but they differ from Imperial doctrine so profoundly that the contradiction cannot be tolerated. Sanguinius holds his counsel, knowing Horus needs his support.

During the nine-week voyage, Abaddon confronts Horus directly, telling him he has no choice. It becomes a shouting match, and Horus threatens to find another first captain before throwing him out. Alone with Loken and Aximand, Horus cools instantly and explains himself.

He recounts his first days with the Emperor, who showed him the stars and said every one could be a human realm free from war and alien tyranny — "Make no mistake, and they will be ours." The Emperor's first gift to him was a child's astrological primer; he memorised all twenty zodiac signs in one evening, and was told that one day twenty sons would match them. The Emperor's favourite was Sagittary, the horse-archer — the perfect synthesis of man and war machine, which is what a Warmaster must be: half man, half army. At Ullanor the Emperor gave him an ancient Persian ring bearing that sign. So Horus is disposed to like the dreadful Sagittary now they meet face to face.

Sanguinius, who has been listening, makes him admit the real reason. *Make no mistake.* Since Ullanor Horus has made two, and as Warmaster he cannot afford them. Sixty-Three Nineteen was blood spilt from misunderstanding, Sejanus dead, and a war he believes could have been avoided. Murder — Urisarach — was a second: the interex left warning beacons and the Imperium walked straight into an embargoed reservation. So he will not make a third rash decision. He will learn from the interex before he judges them. Aximand argues with Abaddon that tolerance is weakness; Horus counters that the interex found a way around annihilation, and asks which of them is the more humane.

The interex are then described. They communicate through the "aria" — a mathematical universal constant expressed as harmony, played by translator-envoys called meturge players. Their soldiers, gleves and sagittars, wear slender silver-green armour and carry spears and recurve bows. Their envoy is Diath Shehn, abbrocarius. They are ancestrally from Terra and treat contact with their lost cousins as momentous — but the word "Warmaster" makes the music go discordant, and Shehn immediately reads it as bellicose.

Then the escorts remove their helmets, and the squat brown-and-gold troopers are revealed as not human at all: the kinebrach, an alien species fifteen thousand years old, defeated by the interex in their first alien war and then absorbed as client citizens rather than exterminated, sharing their technology in exchange. Shehn explains that the megarachnid could not be reasoned with, so they were stripped of interstellar technology and exiled to a reservation world with warning beacons around it — because what right does anyone have to make another species extinct? Abaddon bristles; Horus cuts him off and formally apologises for the Imperium's transgression.

## 19 · Envoys and delegations

Abaddon rages at the apology — six months of war, brothers dead, and now the Warmaster says sorry to xenos-lovers. Aximand says it takes a strong man to know when to apologise; Abaddon answers that only a fool appeases aliens, and challenges anyone to name one species that would not rejoice to see mankind gone. Nobody can. Aximand argues the Mournival must advise with one voice; Loken disagrees — each must say what he thinks and let Horus decide.

The chapter lays out the sheer weight on Horus. Five primarchs want personal audiences, including the Lion for the first time ever. Thirty-six expedition fleets want help. Boniface of the Alpha Legion arrives lobbying for Alpharius's five-year Kayvas Belt offensive, which Horus judges impossible. Worst of all, the eaxector tributi tax administrators arrive under high administrix Aenid Rathbone, demanding tithes from barely-compliant worlds on Malcador's authority. Horus jokes that it will be tax collectors, not eldar or orks, that undo them — then says he is only half joking. He does not believe the Emperor sanctioned this; the Council is making decisions in his absence, and it could trigger uprisings and civil war. He orders Maloghurst to bury Rathbone in paperwork.

Erebus of the Word Bearers is introduced: first chaplain, close to Lorgar and Kor Phaeron, sent to broker something but choosing to shelve his request rather than add to Horus's burden — which wins him the Mournival's admiration completely. In the practice cages he beats Torgaddon, Aximand and Tarvitz, then fights Lucius to a sixteen-minute standstill before Lucius disarms him. Loken then faces Lucius, lets him lunge, side-steps, and simply punches him in the face: "That's how a Luna Wolf fights. Understand your foe and do whatever is necessary to bring him down."

Horus decides to attend the summit. The Imperials travel to Xenobia, deliberately bringing poets, iterators and remembrancers as well as warriors. They are housed in the Extranus, the quarter reserved for strangers, and shown a beautiful, cultured city. At the welcome parade the interex reveal their centaur cavalry — riders fused into headless robotic horses, myth made technological.

The parade ends at the Hall of Devices, a museum and armoury which Aximand reads as a deliberate insult: the interex are as good as saying they have advanced beyond war and retired it as a curiosity. Its curator is a kinebrach called Asherot. Among the confiscated kinebrach weapons, Sindermann asks why some plain daggers are considered too lethal to release. General Commander Naud explains: they are **anathames**, blades of sentient metal, and when one is chosen for use against a specific target it becomes that target's nemesis, utterly inimical to them. Sindermann jokingly asks whether it is a curse or an enchantment — and is unsettled when Naud agrees that is a fair description.

## 20 · Impasse

Three weeks in, the talks have produced nothing but a willingness to trade. Abaddon and Erebus both argue that the interex are too deviant to be tolerated and must be regarded as enemies. Horus refuses to force compliance, and says plainly: "Perhaps the Emperor's model is too stringent." The silence that follows is broken by Sanguinius laughing off the idea that the Warmaster is contemplating defiance. Horus argues the crusade's ruthlessness was forged in an age when every contact was hostile, that two centuries have changed the problem, and that the Emperor trusts him to interpret policy — and to make no mistakes.

Sanguinius departs for Imperial space to buy his brother more time. That night Loken stands perimeter guard at Naud's house while Horus dines there, hoping to informally test whether the interex might recognise the Emperor's authority.

In a hallway Loken finds an ancient Terran book — *A Marvelous Historie of Eevil*, a treatise against sorcery and the seduction of the daemon — and falls into conversation with a royal officer, Mithras Tull, with no meturge player present for the first time. Tull calls it a quaint dark-age piece, then mentions **Kaos** casually, and is astonished to realise Loken has never heard the word used that way.

He explains: Chaos is a primal force residing in the immaterium, the source of all sorcery, the greatest enemy of mankind because it corrupts from within like a disease. The eldar taught the interex the rudiments of it. And this is why they have held the Imperium at arm's length — after an age of strife, men claiming to come from Terra might be agents of Chaos wearing a seemly guise. Chaos when it manifests is brutal, rapacious and warlike; and the Imperium arrives bred for battle, led by a man who calls himself *Warmaster*. "You resemble the enemy we have been raised from birth to anticipate. The all-conquering, unrelenting daemon of Kaos-war." Loken protests, and Tull — convinced — says they have made a mistake by delaying, that there is no taint, only surprising innocence, and offers brotherhood.

Then his comm crackles. He demands Loken's weapons; Loken refuses. Tull accuses him of seduction and bad timing: the Hall of Devices is burning. They fight, two schools of swordsmanship with no common language, until Loken takes Tull's sword-arm off at the elbow and spares his life. Imperial vox is being damped city-wide.

Loken fights his way to the dining hall. Sagittar arrows punch clean through Mark IV plate — the "ceremonial" bows kill several of his men. The bodyguard extracts Horus through the loading docks, losing Oltrentz, Zakes, Cyclos, Regold and Jaeldon.

Outside, Horus refuses to run. Naud accused him of robbery, deceit and murder: someone raided the Hall of Devices, killed Asherot the curator, and stole weapons. Horus insists on going there. Finding it ablaze, a dying meturge player curses them for a killing they had no need to commit. Horus initially concludes the fault lies in the interex — that they must be tainted after all — but Loken argues the opposite: these people hate Chaos as much as the Imperium does, and something tonight has confirmed their fear of *us*.

Horus screams a curse at the stars, and Loken hears him whisper: "Why have you tasked me with this, father? Why have you forsaken me? It is too hard. Why did you leave me to do this on my own?" Then, an arrow in his arm, he picks up a dead man's sword and bolter and leads a last stand in a firelit street. Loken thinks it the fiercest, saddest and most noble battle he has ever fought at his commander's side — a moment that deserved frescoes and symphonies, and that the hateful future would swallow until such nobility became impossible to believe.

## 21 · Parting shots

The battle engulfs the city until dawn. Abaddon and Aximand mobilise the garrison, restore communications, and cut a path to the Warmaster; the fleet moves in and a full extraction is coordinated. Horus sends a final message expressing bitter regret, blaming the interex's heavy hand, and denying every accusation. He gets no reply.

Back in Imperial space he issues his decree: reconsidering the importance of defining his role, the Luna Wolves will henceforth be known as the **Sons of Horus**. The news is celebrated everywhere — Sindermann approves before returning to books nobody has read in a thousand years; Karkasy drinks to it; and Euphrati Keeler kneels at her secret shrine and thanks her god, the Emperor, for giving strong and honourable men to protect them.

Then, in the black bilges of the Vengeful Spirit, by the light of a single candle, a figure examines a rippled grey blade that glitters like diamond — a fine thing, a beautiful thing, a cosmos-changing thing, with the promise and the curse breathing inside it. **Erebus** lowers the anathame into its casket and closes the lid.

Loken tells Mersadie the whole story, and says he has never been more certain that the Emperor chose the right man as Warmaster. She asks what happens next. A month's rendezvous, a campaign in the Caiades Cluster — but first a brief detour to settle a minor dispute, an old tally, at First Chaplain Erebus's request. Where? "A little moon, in the Davin System."

## The main themes, in short

- **Truth as the founding value.** The iterators' whole creed is that the Imperium is right because it is right, not because it is mighty — and the book steadily shows truth being suppressed for morale, for politics, for the Legion's reputation.
- **Secrets and the cost of keeping them.** Jubal is covered up, the lodges hide themselves, Keeler's picts are buried. Loken's own warning: a body that gets very good at keeping secrets will eventually keep any secret at all.
- **Faith returning by the back door.** A crusade fought to abolish religion produces the Lectitio Divinitatus, and Keeler's shrine is lit in the same book that insists there are no gods.
- **Chaos named.** The Imperium's greatest danger is the one thing nobody has told it exists; the interex have to explain it, and are destroyed for the attempt.
- **Horus at his best.** The novel deliberately shows him noble, self-doubting, politically brilliant and desperate not to fail his father — so that what comes later is a fall, not a reveal. The last image is Erebus with the anathame.
